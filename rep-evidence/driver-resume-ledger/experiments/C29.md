# Experiment: C29 — the outage ceiling, and what the ledger design degrades to past it

**Note:** C29 was split out of C28's sweep. The measurement below is the
`outage_60` arm of `runs/20260910-...-C28storm2` plus the follow-up probe
`poc/stage_c/probe_outage_ceiling.py`, whose output is stored in that run
directory. The pre-registration for the sweep itself is C28's card, reproduced
after this header for provenance.

## Claim

- **C29.** A GCS outage at or beyond `gcs_rpc_server_reconnect_timeout_s`
  (60 s default) terminates the **cluster** rather than the coordinator, and the ledger design
  degrades to a cold restart on the same rocksdb path with zero data loss and
  zero redo.
  *Refutes if:* the ledger is lost or corrupted after the cluster dies, **or** a
  cold restart on the same storage path redoes committed work, **or** the epoch
  counter fails to advance for the new incarnation.

## What was observed

The 60 s arm produced no final measurement at all — the follow-up driver could
not even `ray.init`:

    ConnectionError: Failed to connect to Ray cluster at ...:6591

and the sampler had been erroring since t=24 s. The raylet gave up waiting for
GCS and the node exited. So above the ceiling the question "how many times does
the coordinator restart" is not merely unanswered, it is **ill-posed**: there is
no cluster to restart into.

## The probe, and why it is the interesting half

A dead cluster is only a disaster if the ledger dies with it. It does not.
Restarting `ray start --head` on the **same** `gcs_storage_path` and reattaching:

| | |
|---|---|
| epoch keys present | `[2]` |
| committed units recovered | **45**, contiguous, no duplicates |
| ledger key count | 3 |
| actor name still registered | yes — `coord-outage_60` |
| fresh incarnation | `resumed_from=45`, `executed_this_incarnation=0` |
| epoch on a second cold restart | **3** — advances correctly |

Zero redo, twice. The first cold restart reported epoch 2 because GCS had
restored the detached actor from `tables/` and `get_if_exists=True` returned
that same incarnation; the second, after it was stopped, claimed epoch 3. Both
behaviours are correct and they are worth distinguishing, because "restored
incarnation keeps its epoch" is exactly what fencing requires — a restored actor
must **not** claim a second epoch for itself.

## Reading

C29 is the answer to "what is the ledger design's worst case". The failure mode above the
ceiling is not data loss and not redo; it is **downtime**, bounded by how long a
cold restart takes. That is a strictly better degradation than the design
assumed, and it is the direct payoff of putting the ledger in rocksdb rather
than in memory.

## Provenance: C28's pre-registered card


**Pre-registered:** 2026-09-10, iteration 17, before the rig was written.

Found by accident. In `runs/20260910-074504-C26async3` the surviving epoch key
was numbered **18** in one arm and **19** in the other, after a single 35-second
outage. Roughly eighteen incarnations were created where one was expected: the
coordinator died, `max_restarts=-1` brought it back, `__init__` ran recovery
against a GCS that was still unavailable, and the cycle repeated.

Two halves, and they point opposite ways:

- **Encouraging.** Exactly *one* epoch key survived. The compaction sweep (M3)
  held the key-count bound through all of it, which is the property C6 and
  stage B depend on.
- **Concerning.** The epoch counter climbed to 18. If that number scales with
  outage duration rather than sitting at a small constant, then "restartable
  coordinator" plus "GCS outage" composes into unbounded churn.

A claim discovered by accident is exactly the kind that has never been looked at
deliberately, which is why it outranks everything else open.

## Claim

- **C28.** A coordinator that crashes while GCS is unavailable does not thrash:
  with `max_restarts=-1` the restart storm is bounded, and the compaction sweep
  keeps the ledger key count bounded even though the epoch counter climbs.
  *Refutes if:* the ledger key count grows with the number of restarts, **or**
  the epoch counter climbs without bound, **or** repeated failed recoveries redo
  work superlinearly.

Three clauses again. Each is measured separately below; a clause not in the
refutation condition is not being tested.

## The discriminating variable: outage duration

One data point cannot distinguish "18 is a constant" from "18 is proportional to
35 seconds". So sweep the outage and watch the shape:

| arm | outage | role |
|---|---|---|
| `die_healthy` | **0 s** | **negative control** |
| `outage_10` | 10 s | |
| `outage_30` | 30 s | reproduces the accidental observation |
| `outage_60` | 60 s | doubles it |

`die_healthy` is the control and it is load-bearing. It isolates *storm* from
*normal restart*: a coordinator that dies with GCS healthy must come back in
**exactly one** restart and advance the epoch by **exactly one**. If the control
also shows churn, the churn has nothing to do with the outage, the rig is
measuring something else, and the run is **VOID**.

## Measurement

A sampler driver started before the outage polls `progress()` every 2 s and
records `pid`, `epoch`, `resumed_from` and `executed`. Incarnation boundaries are
visible as pid changes, so the sample stream yields:

- **clause 2** — final epoch number and `NumRestarts` from the actor table
- **clause 3** — total redo, summed across incarnations as
  `Σ (executed_at_death − resumed_from)`. This is why the sampler records
  `resumed_from` per incarnation rather than only at the end.
- **clause 1** — ledger key count after recovery, and its trend across arms

The coordinator kills itself via `die()` through the sampler's cached actor
handle, since `ray.kill` needs the GCS actor manager — unavailable mid-outage.

## Prediction (frozen)

1. **Clause 2 will fail: epochs scale with outage duration.** Confidence 0.65.
   Expect roughly one restart every ~2 s of outage, so ≈5 / ≈15 / ≈30 epochs for
   the three arms. If that is what happens, C28 is **refuted** and the design
   needs either a capped `max_restarts`, backoff, or a recovery path that waits
   for GCS instead of dying into a restart.
2. **Clause 1 will hold: key count stays O(1)** — around 3–5 keys regardless of
   restart count, because each new incarnation's startup sweep deletes the
   epochs below it. Confidence 0.8. This is M3's whole purpose and iteration 16
   already showed one surviving key after eighteen incarnations.
3. **Clause 3: redo stays linear, not superlinear.** Confidence 0.6. Each failed
   incarnation should redo at most the units since the last durable commit, and
   an incarnation that cannot reach GCS should execute *nothing*, so most
   restarts ought to cost zero redo. The failure mode worth watching for is an
   incarnation that recovers, executes a batch, dies before committing, and
   repeats — which would make redo grow with restart count.
4. **Control: `die_healthy` shows exactly one restart and epoch +1.**
   Confidence 0.85.

## What each outcome means

| clause 2 | clause 1 | reading |
|---|---|---|
| epochs scale | keys O(1) | **C28 refuted, and usefully.** The ledger is safe but the actor policy is not; the REP must specify restart backoff. |
| epochs ≈ constant | keys O(1) | C28 proven; iteration 16's 18 was an artifact of that particular timing. |
| any | keys grow | **Severe.** M3's sweep does not survive churn, and C6's key bound was proven only under orderly conditions. |
| control churns | — | **VOID.** |

## Fidelity gaps carried in

- Ray's actor restart cadence and any internal backoff are implementation
  details of 2.58 and could differ across versions. The *shape* (constant vs
  proportional) is the durable finding; the constant is not.
