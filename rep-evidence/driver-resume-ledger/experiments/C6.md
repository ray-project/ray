# Experiment: C6

**Pre-registered:** 2026-09-10 — written BEFORE the rig existed and before any
run. Do not edit after running; supersede with a new claim id instead.

## Claim

The M3 compaction protocol is crash-safe over a non-transactional put/del store:
**no crash sequence loses committed progress, and every orphan is removed by a
subsequent instance's sweep.**

Concretely, for every reachable crash schedule with up to `B` crashes over a job
of `N` units with coalescing window `W` and compaction every `K` segments:

- `recover()` returns a superset of every unit whose commit was acked, at every
  point in the trace, not just at the end; and
- after one clean instance completes startup, **zero** keys remain belonging to
  epochs below it, and within the winning epoch at most `K` segment keys plus
  one base plus one pointer remain.

## Refutes if

A crash sequence exists that loses committed progress, or leaves garbage that no
future instance deletes. Either observation kills the claim; the first is fatal
to the design (kill criterion 1 territory), the second is fatal to G3 and forces
a redesign of retention.

## Setup

- Rig: `python3 poc/run_checker.py --experiment C6 --out runs/<id>`
- Fakes in play: `kvfake.py` only. Registered gaps: dict-for-RocksDB (low), no
  real gRPC/latency (low), GCS restart as op-failure not process-restart
  (medium), KV-op scheduling granularity (low). All four are in `claims.json`
  before this run.
- Virtual clock: none needed and none used. C6 is an ordering claim; there is no
  time in the model, so there is no real time source to leak in. Cost is counted
  in fsyncs, not seconds.
- Seeds: not applicable to the exhaustive search — it is a deterministic DFS
  over *all* schedules within the budget, so it has no seed. The randomized deep
  sweep beyond the exhaustive budget uses seeds `[1, 2, 3]`.
- Sweep: crash budget `B` from 1 to 3 (exhaustive at 1 and 2; 3 exhaustive if it
  completes under ~2 min, randomized otherwise). Workload swept over
  `(N, W, K)` = `(6,1,2)`, `(8,2,2)`, `(12,3,3)` so that the trace crosses at
  least two compactions and at least one epoch change.
- Pessimal corner tested: crash placed **between** the two writes of every
  linearization pair — S5/S6 and C1/C2 — and between the pointer flip and the
  delete sweep (C2/C3), in both `before-apply` and `after-apply` phases. Plus
  the carry-forward corner: crash after S6 of epoch `k+1` but before S7, then
  crash again during the *next* instance's carry-forward.

## Prediction (frozen)

1. **The real protocol survives all of it.** Zero INV-DUR and zero INV-PTR
   violations across every exhaustive schedule at `B = 1` and `B = 2`.
   Confidence ~0.6 — this is what `p_wrong = 0.40` on the ledger means, and the
   40% is concentrated in the sweep ordering (S7) and in compaction's delete
   step, not in the pointer flips.
2. **The single most likely place for it to break is S7**, the startup sweep of
   older epochs, because it is the only step that deletes data the current epoch
   has not yet made durable under its own keys. If C6 is refuted, I predict the
   counterexample lands there rather than in compaction.
3. **INV-GC will be violated at least transiently** in essentially every crashed
   trace, and that is expected and fine — the claim is about what survives a
   *subsequent clean startup*, not about the state immediately after a crash.
   The interesting question is whether a trace exists where a clean startup
   still leaves an orphan. I predict it does not, but with lower confidence than
   (1): ~0.7, because the sweep is prefix-scan based and a key written *after*
   the scan but before the delete is a real hazard.
4. **Trace counts** (recorded to catch a rig that is silently exploring nothing):
   `B=1` in the low hundreds; `B=2` in the tens of thousands; `B=3` plausibly
   into the millions and therefore likely to be randomized rather than
   exhaustive.

If the enumerator reports fewer than ~50 completed traces at `B=1`, the rig is
broken, not the design.

## Thresholds

- **PASS:** zero INV-DUR, INV-PTR and INV-GC violations across the full
  exhaustive `B ∈ {1,2}` sweep on all three workload shapes, **and** all four
  C6-relevant negative controls (NC4, NC5, NC6, NC7) produce concrete
  counterexample traces, **and** ≥ 50 traces completed at `B=1`.
- **FAIL:** any INV-DUR or INV-PTR violation on the real protocol, or an INV-GC
  violation that persists after a clean startup.
- **INCONCLUSIVE:** exhaustive search does not complete at `B=2` within the time
  budget, or the state-space counts indicate the enumerator is not reaching
  compaction at all (e.g. zero compactions observed).
- **VOID:** any of NC4–NC7 passes. A control that passes means the checker
  cannot see the failure class it is supposed to see, and the green result on
  the real protocol is then worth nothing.

## Negative controls

| control | how it breaks the mechanism | expected result |
|---|---|---|
| NC4 | startup sweep (S7) moved **before** the carry-forward pointer flip (S6) | INV-DUR RED — a crash between them loses the carried state entirely |
| NC5 | compaction deletes segments before flipping `base_ptr` (C3 before C2) | INV-DUR RED — a crash in the window strands committed units |
| NC6 | compaction overwrites a single `base` key in place instead of writing a fresh `base@m` | INV-PTR or INV-DUR RED — `base_ptr` names a base whose content has already moved |
| NC7 | startup sweep omitted entirely | INV-GC RED — orphans from a crashed epoch survive a clean startup |

A negative control that PASSES voids the run: the rig cannot see failure.

Note that NC6 is the weakest of the four and may turn out to be *safe* in this
model, because a single blind put is atomic in the fake. If NC6 passes I will
**not** treat the run as void on NC6 alone — but I will say so explicitly and
record why, rather than quietly reclassifying it after seeing the result. The
run's validity rests on NC4, NC5 and NC7.

## Result (filled in after the run)

- Run id: `runs/20260910-041339-C6` (git `4eee3fd`)
- Outcome: **proven**

**Observed vs predicted.**

| prediction | outcome |
|---|---|
| (1) zero INV-DUR / INV-PTR at B=1 and B=2 | held — zero violations of any kind across **16,489** exhaustive traces, 3 workload shapes, budgets 1 and 2 |
| (2) if refuted, the counterexample lands in the S7 sweep | not refuted, but the intuition was right: NC4 (sweep moved before the pointer flip) is the most destructive control in the set — it loses the *entire* carried state |
| (3) INV-GC violated transiently but never after a clean startup | held; every crashed trace was swept clean by the audit instance |
| (4) B=1 low hundreds, B=2 tens of thousands | B=1 = 105/80/70 ✓. **B=2 = 7,524/4,731/3,979 — under by ~2-3x.** |
| ≥ 50 traces at B=1 | 70–105 ✓ |

**Surprises, in increasing order of importance.**

1. *(minor)* The B=2 trace count came in below prediction. Mechanism: a crash
   truncates its instance, so injecting a fault early deletes every later
   position in that instance from the search space. The tree is much narrower
   near the root than `|ops|²` suggests. Not a rig defect — the enumeration is
   still complete over reachable plans — but worth writing down so a future
   iteration does not read a small trace count as a broken search.

2. **NC6 — compaction overwriting a single `base` key in place — is GREEN**,
   across 6,513 traces. The card flagged this as possible and pre-committed to
   not voiding the run on it, which is the only reason this is reportable
   rather than embarrassing. Mechanism: the ledger's state is a **grow-only
   set**, so re-deriving it from an older base plus surviving segments is
   idempotent; and with epoch-scoped keys there is no second writer in the
   sequential model to race the overwrite. **The design doc recommends unique
   `base@n` keys and gives crash-safety as the reason. That reason is wrong.**
   Uniqueness may still be right, but if so it earns its keep against a
   *concurrent stale writer*, not against a crash. Raised as **C15**.

3. **The most important finding is about the shape of the risk, not the result.**
   Five of the eight controls (NC4, NC5, NC7, NC10, NC11) go red with **zero
   faults injected**. The bugs they model are not subtle crash-window races —
   they are unconditionally broken, and any competent integration test would
   catch them. The only failure mode that genuinely requires a crash to observe
   is **redo** (NC12), and the only one that requires a lost ack is a **liveness**
   failure in fencing (NC1b). So M3's ordering discipline is *cheap to get right
   and easy to verify*, and the design's residual risk is not where `p_wrong =
   0.40` said it was. It is concentrated in concurrency (C1, C13, C15) and in
   resume efficiency (C2), not in compaction.

**Envelope / breaking point.** No breaking point found within the tested
envelope. What was tested: single-writer, sequential restarts, ≤ 2 faults,
N ∈ {6, 8, 12}, W ∈ {1, 2, 3}, K ∈ {2, 3}, up to 4 coordinator instances and 3
compactions per trace. What was **not** tested and must not be read as covered:
concurrent instances of any kind, ≥ 3 simultaneous faults, and everything behind
fidelity gap F3 (the GCS actor registry across restart is not modelled at all).

**Incidental observations** (not pre-registered, so they move priors and nothing
else): steady-state key count was **3** for N = 6, 8 and 12 alike, which is
direct evidence for C2's "independent of N" clause; and INV-REDO (redo ≤
crashes × W) never fired in any trace, which is evidence for C2's redo-window
clause. C2 stays `untested`; its `p_wrong` drops 0.35 → 0.15.

|---|---|---|
| C2 | p_wrong 0.35 → 0.15 | key count independent of N observed directly; redo bound never violated |
